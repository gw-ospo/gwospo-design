# The four design principles

Robin Williams, *The Non-Designer's Design Book* — **proximity, alignment, repetition,
contrast**. Work through all four after the squint test: they interact, but they fail
independently.

---

## 1. Proximity

> Items related to each other should be grouped close together. Space goes **between**
> groups, not inside them.

**What to look for**
- Even spacing from top to bottom. If a sub-point sits as far from its parent as from the
  next topic, the groups do not exist for the reader.
- Captions floating equidistant between two images, belonging to neither.
- Headings closer to the paragraph *above* them than the one they introduce — a very common
  slip, and it inverts the meaning.
- Boxes, rules, or background tints doing work that spacing should be doing. If you need a
  box to show that four things belong together, try moving them closer first.
- Too many separate groups. Five or six clusters on a page is usually the ceiling before
  the reader stops seeing structure.

**Diagnostic question.** Cover the text and look only at the blocks of grey. Do the clumps
match the outline of the content?

**Fix pattern.** Halve the space inside a group, double the space between groups. Then
delete the box you no longer need.

---

## 2. Alignment

> Nothing should be placed on the page arbitrarily. Every element should have a visual
> connection to something else on the page.

**What to look for**
- Multiple left edges. Headings, body copy, images, and captions each starting a few
  millimetres apart is the single most common cause of "it looks off but I can't say why".
- Centred everything. Centring is a legitimate choice but a weak default: it produces soft,
  invisible edges and no strong line for the eye to follow. A strong left (or right) edge is
  almost always more confident.
- Mixed alignment without reason — a centred headline over left-aligned body.
- Elements aligned to the *page* rather than to each other, or vice versa, inconsistently.
- Optical vs mechanical alignment: a large quotation mark or a round shape needs to hang
  slightly outside the margin to *look* aligned.

**Diagnostic question.** Can you draw a small number of straight lines through the page and
have every element touch one? If you need eight lines, there is no alignment.

**Fix pattern.** Choose one margin and commit every element to it. Establishing a strong
edge is also what earns you the right to break it later — a full-bleed photo only reads as
a decision when everything else is locked to the grid.

---

## 3. Repetition

> Repeat some aspect of the design throughout the entire piece. Repetition is what makes
> separate pages read as one document.

**What to look for**
- **Repeating the logo instead of the system.** This is the classic GW OSCON failure. A mark
  placed on every page to fill a corner stops being a mark and becomes wallpaper — and it
  burns the impact of the one placement that mattered. Repeat *structure*: a running head, a
  motif, a caption style, a heading cascade. See `brand-checks.md` L5.
- Inconsistency where consistency was intended: three caption styles, four heading sizes
  that differ by 1 pt, bullets that change indent between pages.
- Nothing repeating at all, so pages feel unrelated.
- Repetition with no meaning — a decorative element on every page that encodes nothing.

**A test for structural devices.** Numbered markers (01 / 02 / 03), eyebrows, dividers, and
tabs should encode something true. Number a list only if the order actually matters. If the
four items are a set rather than a sequence, numbering them is decoration pretending to be
information.

**Diagnostic question.** Name the three elements that repeat. If you cannot, there is no
system — only a series of pages.

**Fix pattern.** Pick two or three deliberate repeating elements and apply them everywhere.
Write them down so the next person can follow them.

---

## 4. Contrast

> If two items are not exactly the same, then make them very different. Timid difference
> reads as a mistake.

**What to look for**
- Type sizes too close together. A heading at 14 pt over body at 12 pt reads as an error,
  not a hierarchy. Aim for a real jump; a 2–3× ratio between display and body is normal.
- Bold as the only tool. If every emphasis is bold, nothing is emphasised.
- No dominant ground. If dark blue covers most of the page, nothing can stand out against
  it. Emphasis needs a quiet field to land on. See `brand-checks.md` C3.
- Contrast in size only. The strongest pairings differ in **form** as well: Gazzetta is
  narrow, heavy, and newspaper-derived; Avenir Next is round, geometric, and open. That
  difference in shape carries hierarchy even at similar sizes.
- Colour used decoratively rather than semantically. Colour should mark what something *is*
  — the tier being sold, the status that needs attention — not just brighten a corner.
- Competing weights. Thick rules that out-weigh the headline they sit under are fighting the
  type instead of serving it. Hairlines and thin colour caps do the same job quietly.

**Diagnostic question.** Convert the page to greyscale and squint. Is there still a clear
first, second, and third? If the hierarchy vanishes without colour, it was never built.

**Fix pattern.** Double the difference you first reached for — and check it was not then
undone by making everything else loud too.

---

## 5. The thing behind all four: white space

None of the four principles can operate without room. When a layout is crowded, the useful
advice is almost never "add a graphic" or "make it smaller". It is **give it more room** —
cut content, or add a page. A six-page document that wants to be eight pages is not a
design problem with a design solution; it is a pagination decision that unlocks all four
principles at once.

Students are reluctant to spend white space, so expect to argue for it.
